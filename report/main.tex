\begin{abstract}
\noindent Because daily returns are conditionally heteroskedastic, a volatility control chart must specify the reference
against which each return is judged. This study compares three one-sided exponentially weighted moving
average (EWMA) charts that monitor the squared return divided by a variance denominator computed from past returns: a
constant training variance (conventional chart), a frozen GARCH(1,1)-$t$ filter, and a frozen random forest (RF)
trained on lagged squared returns (the proposed RF--EWMA chart). All limits are calibrated by simulation to a common in-control
run-length target of 500 days and validated on independent paths. In a Monte Carlo study of a
GARCH(1,1)-$t$ process whose variance increases persistently by a factor of 1.5, 2 or 3, twenty independently trained
and calibrated systems were compared on common random numbers. The conventional chart detected fastest, with restricted
mean delays of 205, 132 and 63 days, against 243, 192 and 126 for RF--EWMA and 249, 202 and 140 for GARCH--EWMA,
although its variance forecasts were the least accurate. RF--EWMA was on average 6--13 days faster than GARCH--EWMA,
but this advantage did not survive a post hoc multiplicity adjustment, varied strongly between fitted systems and did
not extend to early detection. This is consistent with adaptation masking: adaptive denominators absorb part of a
persistent change. An application to SPDR S\&P~500 ETF returns from 2007 to 2026 illustrates how the
charts flag different periods of market stress. More accurate variance forecasts did not give faster detection of
persistent volatility increases.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} EWMA control chart; random forest; GARCH; conditional heteroskedasticity; volatility
monitoring; run length; adaptation masking.

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
Volatility is a central input of financial risk measurement, and a persistent increase in it changes the risk of every
position exposed to the market. Daily returns are close to serially uncorrelated, but their squares are not: large moves
cluster, a conditional heteroskedasticity captured by the generalised autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity
(GARCH) model of \citet{bollerslev_1986}. Statistical process control (SPC) offers sequential tools for detecting such
changes, with a run-length interpretation of false alarms and detection delay. The exponentially weighted moving
average (EWMA) chart of \citet{roberts_1959} accumulates evidence over time and is sensitive to small, persistent shifts
\citep{lucas_saccucci_1990}. Classical charts, however, assume independent and identically distributed observations, and
applied directly to financial series they can signal too often: \citet{dumicic_zmuk_2015} charted the prices of a
Croatian stock index with individuals, moving-range, EWMA and CUSUM charts and obtained many signals, which they regarded
mostly as false alarms.

For serially dependent data, \citet{alwan_roberts_1988} proposed fitting a time-series model and charting its one-step
prediction errors, so that the chart monitors departures from the predictable structure rather than the structure
itself. Their proposal concerns the process mean, as does the later work on EWMA charts for autocorrelated processes
\citep{lu_reynolds_1999}; the exponentially weighted moving variance of \citet{macgregor_harris_1993} is a related
statistic for process variability. For the variance of financial returns, \citet{schipper_schmid_2001} developed EWMA
charts for GARCH processes based on squared observations, on the conditional variance and on residuals, the squared
observation divided by its conditional variance. Under known in-control parameters, normal innovations and a short
in-control run length, their residual chart was the best chart in nearly all the cases they studied.

Machine learning (ML) has entered both fields. For forecasting realised variance, \citet{christensen_etal_2023} found
regularised regression, bagging, random forests, gradient boosting and neural networks competitive with heterogeneous
autoregressive (HAR) benchmarks. A random forest \citep{breiman_2001} averages regression trees, so its predictions are
averages of training targets: they are positive and cannot exceed the largest values seen in training. In SPC, random
forests have mainly served as classifiers inside charts, as in the real-time-contrasts chart of \citet{lee_etal_2020}.
Here the random forest is used instead as the variance forecaster that standardises the statistic of a classical EWMA
chart.

Standardising by a forecast creates a tension. A chart that divides each squared return by a forecast built from recent
returns judges every return relative to the recent level of volatility. If that level rises persistently, the forecast
follows it and absorbs part of the change the chart is meant to detect, an effect this paper calls adaptation masking.
A more accurate forecaster may therefore detect persistent increases more slowly, while a random forest, which cannot
extrapolate, may adapt less. Within the literature reviewed here, constant, GARCH and random-forest standardisation have
not been compared within one EWMA design at a common, independently validated in-control run length.

This study asks four questions. Can a random forest trained on lagged squared returns produce positive, accurate one-step
variance forecasts, and how close are they to those of a fitted GARCH model? At a common validated in-control run
length, how do the conventional, GARCH- and RF-standardised EWMA charts compare in detecting a persistent variance
increase? How much of a persistent change does each chart still see after its forecaster adapts? And how do the charts
behave on daily returns of the SPDR S\&P~500 ETF Trust (SPY) from 2007 to 2026? The contributions are a like-for-like
comparison in which only the variance denominator differs; a replicated design with twenty independently trained and
calibrated monitoring systems, which separates Monte Carlo error from variation between fitted systems; and evidence
that forecast accuracy and detection speed can rank the charts in opposite orders. Section~\ref{sec:background} reviews
the charts and the random forest, Section~\ref{sec:proposed} specifies the monitoring scheme and the simulation design,
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports and discusses the simulation results, Section~\ref{sec:spy} presents the SPY
application and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Mathematical background}\label{sec:background}
\subsection{Existing charts}\label{sec:existing}
Let $P_t$ be the closing price of an asset on trading day $t$, and let
\begin{equation}
r_t = 100\ln(P_t/P_{t-1}) \label{eq:ret}
\end{equation}
be the percentage log return, the convention of \citet{bollerslev_1986}. The information available before day $t$ is
$\F_{t-1}$, generated by the returns up to day $t-1$, and every forecast used on day $t$ must be $\F_{t-1}$-measurable.

\paragraph{The EWMA chart.} For observations $x_t$ with in-control mean $\mu_0$, the EWMA statistic of
\citet{roberts_1959} is
\begin{equation}
Z_t = \lambda x_t + (1-\lambda) Z_{t-1}, \qquad Z_0=\mu_0,\quad 0<\lambda\le 1. \label{eq:ewma0}
\end{equation}
Unrolled, $Z_t=\lambda\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}(1-\lambda)^j x_{t-j}+(1-\lambda)^t\mu_0$, a weighted average whose weights decline
geometrically; a small $\lambda$ gives a long memory, which suits small, persistent shifts \citep{lucas_saccucci_1990}.
If the $x_t$ are independent with variance $\sigma_x^2$,
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{Var}(Z_t)=\sigma_x^2\,\frac{\lambda}{2-\lambda}\bigl[1-(1-\lambda)^{2t}\bigr], \label{eq:ewmavar}
\end{equation}
and the classical limits $\mu_0\pm L\sigma_x\sqrt{\lambda/(2-\lambda)}$ follow by treating $Z_t$ as approximately
normal. A one-sided upper chart, appropriate when only increases matter, signals at the run length
\begin{equation}
T=\inf\{t\ge 1: Z_t>h\} \label{eq:rl}
\end{equation}
for a control limit $h$. The in-control average run length $\mathrm{ARL}_0=\E_0(T)$ measures the false-alarm rate, and
the run length after a change measures the detection delay. Charts should be compared at a common in-control level, as
\citet{roberts_1959} already recommended. For independent observations the run-length distribution can be computed by
discretising $Z_t$ into a Markov chain \citep{brook_evans_1972}, an approach that \citet{lucas_saccucci_1990} applied
to EWMA schemes; for normal data they found the in-control run length nearly geometric. None of these results applies
directly to squared financial returns, which are neither independent nor normal.

\paragraph{GARCH(1,1) with Student-$t$ innovations.} Volatility clustering is modelled by
\begin{equation}
r_t=\sqrt{h_t}\,\eta_t,\qquad h_t=\omega+\alpha r_{t-1}^2+\beta h_{t-1}, \label{eq:garch}
\end{equation}
with $\omega>0$ and $\alpha,\beta\ge0$ \citep{bollerslev_1986}. Here the innovations $\eta_t=\sqrt{(\nu-2)/\nu}\,\xi_t$,
with independent Student-$t_\nu$ variables $\xi_t$ and $\nu>2$, have unit variance and heavier tails than the normal
innovations of the original model. The conditional variance $h_t$ is $\F_{t-1}$-measurable. The process is covariance
stationary if and only if $\alpha+\beta<1$, with unconditional variance $\sigma^2=\omega/(1-\alpha-\beta)$
\citep[Theorem~1]{bollerslev_1986}, and the persistence $\alpha+\beta$ governs how slowly volatility shocks decay. Squaring
$h_t$ and taking expectations shows that the fourth moment of $r_t$ is finite if and only if
\begin{equation}
\beta^2+2\alpha\beta+\kappa_\eta\alpha^2<1,\qquad \kappa_\eta=\E\eta_t^4=\frac{3(\nu-2)}{\nu-4}\quad(\nu>4),
\label{eq:m4}
\end{equation}
which reduces to the condition $3\alpha^2+2\alpha\beta+\beta^2<1$ of \citet[Theorem~2]{bollerslev_1986} for normal
innovations ($\kappa_\eta=3$). The left-hand side of \eqref{eq:m4} is called the fourth-moment coefficient below.

\paragraph{Charts for the variance of a GARCH process.} Among the charts of \citet{schipper_schmid_2001} are EWMA charts
of the squared observation and of the residual $(X_t-\mu_0)^2/\hat\sigma_t^2$, where $\hat\sigma_t^2$ is the best linear
predictor of $X_t^2$ under the known in-control parameters; the residual chart starts at 1. With a zero mean, both monitor
a score of the form
\begin{equation}
u_t=r_t^2/d_t, \label{eq:score0}
\end{equation}
with a constant denominator $d_t=\sigma^2$ or a forecast $d_t\approx h_t$, and the choice determines what the chart
sees. With a constant denominator, $\E(u_t\mid\F_{t-1})=h_t/\sigma^2$, so $u_t$ inherits the volatility clustering and is
serially dependent. With the true conditional variance, $u_t=\eta_t^2$ is independent and identically distributed with
mean 1, but it is far from normal; for $\nu=6$, $\operatorname{Var}(\eta_t^2)=\kappa_\eta-1=5$. In neither case are
normal-theory limits based on \eqref{eq:ewmavar} justified, which is why the limits in this study are calibrated by
simulation (Section~\ref{sec:calib}).

\paragraph{A persistent change in volatility.} Following \citet{schipper_schmid_2001} with a pure scale change, the
observed returns are
\begin{equation}
r_t^{\mathrm{obs}}=\begin{cases} r_t, & t<\tau,\\ \sqrt{\delta}\,r_t, & t\ge\tau, \end{cases} \label{eq:fault}
\end{equation}
for a change point $\tau$ and a variance multiplier $\delta>1$. By \eqref{eq:garch}, the changed process is again
GARCH(1,1), with $\omega$ replaced by $\delta\omega$ and unconditional variance $\delta\sigma^2$: the change raises the
level of volatility permanently and leaves its dynamics unchanged.

\subsection{Machine learning}\label{sec:ml}
\paragraph{Prediction target.} The denominator in \eqref{eq:score0} should be the one-step conditional variance. A
regression of $r_t^2$ on past information instead estimates
\begin{equation}
\E(r_t^2\mid\F_{t-1})=\operatorname{Var}(r_t\mid\F_{t-1})+\{\E(r_t\mid\F_{t-1})\}^2, \label{eq:target}
\end{equation}
which equals the conditional variance only when the conditional mean is zero. The simulated returns have conditional
mean zero, so the two coincide. The SPY returns are demeaned by their training mean (Section~\ref{sec:spy}); any
remaining conditional mean adds its square to the target, a term that is small relative to the variance of daily returns
but not zero. The target $y_t=r_t^2$ is conditionally unbiased for $h_t$ but very noisy: under \eqref{eq:garch},
$r_t^2=h_t\eta_t^2$, whose conditional coefficient of variation is $\sqrt{\kappa_\eta-1}\approx2.2$ for $\nu=6$.

\paragraph{Features.} Following the HAR structure that \citet{christensen_etal_2023} use for realised variance, the
forecaster uses the daily, weekly and monthly averages of past squared returns,
\begin{equation}
\bm x_t=\Bigl(r_{t-1}^2,\ \tfrac15\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{5}r_{t-j}^2,\ \tfrac1{22}\sum_{j=1}^{22}r_{t-j}^2\Bigr).
\label{eq:features}
\end{equation}
All three are $\F_{t-1}$-measurable, so a forecast for day $t$ never uses $r_t$. Only magnitudes enter, so the forest,
like the symmetric GARCH(1,1), ignores the signs of past returns.

\paragraph{Random forest regression.} A regression tree partitions the feature space into rectangular leaves by recursive
binary splits that reduce the within-node squared error, and predicts the mean training target of the leaf that
contains $\bm x$. A random forest \citep{breiman_2001} averages $B$ such trees $T_b$, each grown on a bootstrap sample of
the training pairs $(\bm x_i,y_i)$ and choosing every split from a random subset of $m$ features. Because each leaf value
is a mean of training targets,
\begin{equation}
\hat f(\bm x)=\frac1B\sum_{b=1}^{B}T_b(\bm x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_i(\bm x)\,y_i,\qquad w_i(\bm x)\ge0,\quad
\sum_{i=1}^n w_i(\bm x)=1, \label{eq:rf}
\end{equation}
where $w_i(\bm x)$ is the tree-averaged bootstrap weight of observation $i$ in the leaves containing $\bm x$. This form has
two consequences for variance forecasting. First, every prediction is nonnegative, and positive unless all targets in
the relevant leaves are zero; the floor
\begin{equation}
d_t^{RF}=\max\{\hat f(\bm x_t),\,c\,s^2\},\qquad c=10^{-4}, \label{eq:floor}
\end{equation}
with $s^2$ the training variance, guards the denominator, and the number of times it binds is recorded. Second,
$\hat f(\bm x)$ never exceeds the largest leaf mean: the forest does not extrapolate, so when volatility rises beyond
the training range its forecasts saturate. \citet{breiman_2001} shows that the generalisation error of a forest
converges as $B\to\infty$ and is bounded by the mean correlation between the trees' residuals times the error of a single
tree. His theory assumes independent training data; daily returns are dependent, so out-of-bag error is not used here.
The forest is tuned on separate data instead, an independent path in the simulation and a later period for SPY, in the
spirit of the chronological validation of \citet{christensen_etal_2023}.

\paragraph{What the forest estimates.} The features are a coarsening of $\F_{t-1}$, so even a perfectly fitted forest
estimates $m(\bm x)=\E(h_t\mid\bm x_t=\bm x)$ rather than $h_t$. For this best predictor
$\operatorname{cov}\{m(\bm x_t),h_t\}=\operatorname{var}\{m(\bm x_t)\}$, so the slope of the forecast on the true
variance,
\begin{equation}
b=\operatorname{cov}(d_t,h_t)/\operatorname{var}(h_t), \label{eq:slope}
\end{equation}
is at most one for $d_t=m(\bm x_t)$, with equality only if $h_t$ is a function of $\bm x_t$. A slope below one, a
forecast that moves less than proportionally with the true variance, can thus arise from limited information alone, as
well as from estimation error, regularisation by large leaves or saturation.

\paragraph{Evaluating variance forecasts.} Forecasts $d_t$ are compared with the QLIKE and squared-error losses
\begin{equation}
L_{\mathrm{QLIKE}}(p,d)=\log d+p/d,\qquad L_{\mathrm{MSE}}(p,d)=(p-d)^2, \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
where $p$ is the true variance or a proxy for it. \citet{patton_2011} shows that both losses are robust to noise in a
conditionally unbiased proxy such as $r_t^2$: ranking forecasts by expected loss against the proxy gives the same ranking
as against the true conditional variance. In the simulation the true $h_t$ is known and used; on SPY only the proxy is
available, so QLIKE values from the two settings are not comparable in level. The fraction of the QLIKE gap between a
constant forecast ($C$) and the known-parameter GARCH filter ($O$) that a forecaster closes,
\begin{equation}
G=(\bar L_{C}-\bar L)/(\bar L_{C}-\bar L_{O}), \label{eq:gap}
\end{equation}
where $\bar L$ denotes mean QLIKE against $h_t$, summarises forecast accuracy on a scale from 0 (no better than a
constant) to 1 (as good as the true in-control model).

\section{Proposed RF--EWMA monitoring scheme}\label{sec:proposed}
\subsection{Monitoring score and charts}\label{sec:charts}
The three charts monitor the same score and differ only in its denominator. For chart $k$ with an $\F_{t-1}$-measurable
denominator $d_t^{(k)}$, the score and the one-sided EWMA statistic are
\begin{align}
u_t^{(k)} &= r_t^2/d_t^{(k)}, \label{eq:score}\\
Z_t^{(k)} &= \lambda u_t^{(k)} + (1-\lambda) Z_{t-1}^{(k)}, \qquad Z_0^{(k)} = 1,\quad 0<\lambda\le 1, \label{eq:ewma}
\end{align}
and the chart signals on the first day with $Z_t^{(k)}>h_k^*$, its calibrated limit. The starting value 1 is the
in-control mean of a correctly standardised score. The denominators are:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
\item \emph{Conventional EWMA}: $d_t^{C}=s^2=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n r_i^2$, the mean squared return of the $n$ training
returns (the squared-observation chart);
\item \emph{GARCH--EWMA}: $d_t^{G}=\hat h_t=\hat\omega+\hat\alpha r_{t-1}^2+\hat\beta\hat h_{t-1}$, the GARCH(1,1) filter
with $(\hat\omega,\hat\alpha,\hat\beta,\hat\nu)$ estimated once by Student-$t$ maximum likelihood with zero mean (the
residual chart of \citet{schipper_schmid_2001}, here with estimated rather than known parameters);
\item \emph{RF--EWMA} (proposed): $d_t^{RF}$ of \eqref{eq:floor}, with the forest fitted once to the training pairs
$(\bm x_i,r_i^2)$.
\end{itemize}
All three forecasters are frozen after training: $s^2$, the GARCH parameters and the trees never change, while the
filter state $\hat h_t$ and the features $\bm x_t$ update daily from past returns. Monitoring is preceded by a history of
$W=250$ days that initialises the filter and the features; because $\hat\beta^{W}\approx0$, the filter's starting value
is immaterial, and monitoring days are counted from the first day after this history. In the simulation a fourth
denominator, the GARCH filter with the true in-control parameters, serves as an internal diagnostic, the
\emph{oracle}. It equals $h_t$ in control, apart from a negligible initialisation error, but it does not know about the
change: it is the best forecaster of the in-control process, not a fourth competing chart.

\subsection{Adaptation masking}\label{sec:masking}
On the day of a change every denominator is still unaffected, because $d_\tau$ uses only returns before $\tau$, so
$\E(u_\tau\mid\F_{\tau-1})=\delta h_\tau/d_\tau$ and the full shift is visible. Afterwards an adaptive denominator rises
with the new volatility level: the GARCH filter geometrically at rate $\hat\beta$ per day, and the forest through its
1-, 5- and 22-day features, but only up to its saturation level. Comparing the stationary means of the filter before and
after the change gives a first-order approximation to the long-run ratio of mean GARCH scores,
\begin{equation}
R_G(\delta)=\frac{\delta(\hat\omega+\hat\alpha\sigma^2)}{\hat\omega+\hat\alpha\delta\sigma^2}<\delta, \label{eq:RG}
\end{equation}
whereas a constant denominator keeps the ratio at exactly $\delta$. To measure the fraction of a shift that remains
visible, let $\bar u_k(\delta)$ be the mean score $k$ days after the change, computed on the same paths with the change
and, for $\delta=1$, without it, and let $\bar u_S$ be its average over the sustained window $k\in[100,499]$. The
masking ratio
\begin{equation}
M=\frac{\bar u_S(\delta)/\bar u_S(1)-1}{\delta-1} \label{eq:masking}
\end{equation}
equals 1 for the conventional chart by construction and $\{R_G(\delta)-1\}/(\delta-1)$ under the approximation
\eqref{eq:RG}; smaller values mean that more of the shift has been absorbed.

\subsection{Control limits: calibration and independent validation}\label{sec:calib}
Because the scores are serially dependent, skewed and heavy-tailed, the limits are calibrated empirically. For $N$
simulated in-control paths, each monitored for at most $T_{\max}$ days, the restricted mean run length
\begin{equation}
\hat A(h)=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\min\{T_i(h),T_{\max}\} \label{eq:arlhat}
\end{equation}
is a lower bound for the mean run length and nondecreasing in $h$, since raising the limit can only delay the alarm on
every path. The calibrated limit is the smallest value that attains the target $A_0$,
\begin{equation}
h^*=\min\{h:\hat A(h)\ge A_0\}, \label{eq:hstar}
\end{equation}
found by bisection with $A_0=500$ days, $N=2000$ and $T_{\max}=5000$. The limit is then fixed and checked on 2,000
independent in-control paths. It is accepted when the 95\% confidence interval
$[\widehat A_{\mathrm{lo}},\widehat A_{\mathrm{hi}}]=\widehat A_{\mathrm{val}}\mp1.96\,\mathrm{SE}$ of the validation
estimate lies within a practical tolerance of the target,
\begin{equation}
\bigl[\widehat{A}_{\mathrm{lo}},\,\widehat{A}_{\mathrm{hi}}\bigr] \subseteq \bigl[A_0(1-\varepsilon),\,A_0(1+\varepsilon)\bigr],
\qquad A_0=500,\ \varepsilon=0.15, \label{eq:val}
\end{equation}
that is, within [425, 575]. The rule asks whether the in-control run length is demonstrably within $\pm15\%$ of the
target, not whether it equals 500. It is conditional on the calibrated $h^*$, so it ignores the uncertainty of $h^*$
itself, and it was adopted after a pilot study and a benchmark run, before the main simulation. A failed validation was to be reported,
never repaired by recalibration.

\subsection{Simulation design}\label{sec:design}
The in-control process is \eqref{eq:garch} with $\omega=0.02$, $\alpha=0.07$, $\beta=0.91$ and $\nu=6$: unconditional
variance 1, persistence 0.98 and fourth-moment coefficient 0.985, so that $\E r_t^4$ is finite although the returns are
strongly leptokurtic (kurtosis about 15.7). Since every denominator scales with the squared returns, the unit variance
involves no loss of generality. Each of $R=20$ independent replicates, or monitoring systems, comprises
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
\item a training path of $n=3500$ days, to which $s^2$, the GARCH model and the forests are fitted;
\item an independent development path of 3,500 days, on which the forest is chosen from a grid of minimum leaf sizes
$\{50,200,800\}$ and features per split $m\in\{1,3\}$, each with $B=500$ trees, by the lowest mean QLIKE against
$r_t^2$;
\item 2,000 calibration and 2,000 validation paths for every chart and smoothing constant;
\item 2,000 out-of-control paths with a change \eqref{eq:fault} on monitoring day $\tau=50$, monitored for $H=500$ days
after the change.
\end{enumerate}
Each role draws from its own random stream, so no path is used for two purposes. The out-of-control paths use common
random numbers: the same innovations generate the path for every $\delta\in\{1,1.5,2,3\}$ and are monitored by every
chart, so the charts are compared on identical data and $\delta=1$ gives a no-change counterpart of each path. The large
leaf sizes reflect a target $r_t^2$ that is far noisier than the realised variance for which
\citet{christensen_etal_2023} use a minimum node size of 5. The smoothing constant $\lambda=0.1$ is primary and
$\lambda=0.2$ secondary; neither was tuned on out-of-control results.

\subsection{Performance measures and inference}\label{sec:measures}
A path that signals before the change ($T<\tau$) has a pre-change false alarm. Otherwise the detection delay is
$D=T-\tau+1$, restricted to $D_H=\min(D,H)$ because some paths do not signal within $H$ days. The primary delay measure
is the restricted mean over the paths that survive to the change,
\begin{equation}
\RMD=\frac1{|E|}\sum_{i\in E}\min(D_i,500),\qquad E=\{i:T_i\ge\tau\}, \label{eq:rmd}
\end{equation}
a lower bound for the conditional mean delay that should not be read as an unrestricted out-of-control ARL. Early
detection is measured by the excess detection within $k$ days,
\begin{equation}
X(k)=\frac1{|E|}\sum_{i\in E}\bigl[\mathbf 1\{D_i(\delta)\le k\}-\mathbf 1\{D_i(1)\le k\}\bigr], \label{eq:xk}
\end{equation}
which subtracts the alarms the same path would have produced without the change and is zero at $\delta=1$ by
construction.

Two charts $a$ and $b$ are compared on the paths that survive to the change for both, $E_a\cap E_b$, by the mean paired
difference of $D_H$ or of the excess-detection indicators. Within a replicate such a contrast is conditional on the
fitted forecasters and calibrated limits. The method-level estimand is the mean contrast over independently trained
systems, estimated by the equal-weight mean $\bar\theta$ of the replicate estimates $\hat\theta_r$ with the interval
\begin{equation}
\bar\theta\pm t_{19,\,0.975}\,s_b/\sqrt{R},\qquad s_b^2=\frac1{R-1}\sum_{r=1}^R(\hat\theta_r-\bar\theta)^2,
\label{eq:tci}
\end{equation}
whose width reflects the variation between fitted systems as well as Monte Carlo error. Twelve primary contrasts were
pre-specified before the main simulation: the differences RF$-$GARCH and RF$-$conventional in $\RMD$ and in $X(20)$
at $\delta\in\{1.5,2,3\}$ and $\lambda=0.1$, each reported with its $\delta=1$ counterpart. No multiplicity adjustment was
pre-specified, so their intervals are unadjusted. A Holm step-down adjustment over the twelve contrasts
\citep{holm_1979}, which controls the family-wise error rate without assumptions about the dependence between tests, was
added after the results had been seen and is reported only as a supplementary check. All other quantities are secondary
or descriptive.

\paragraph{Forecasts.} The random forest's forecasts were positive and finite throughout; the floor \eqref{eq:floor}
never bound in $2\times10^8$ forecasts. Their accuracy lay between that of the constant and that of GARCH (Table~\ref{tab:sim}):
mean QLIKE against the true variance was 0.841, against 0.812 for GARCH, 0.810 for the oracle and 1.019 for the
constant, so the forest closed 85\% of the gap \eqref{eq:gap} ($G=0.850$ [0.827, 0.873]), against 98.9\% for GARCH. The
forecasts moved much less than the true variance (slope \eqref{eq:slope} 0.35, against 1.00 for GARCH; correlation with
$h_t$ 0.64), and the RF scores were almost uncorrelated (lag-1 autocorrelation 0.011, against 0.132 for the constant).

\paragraph{In-control calibration.} All 160 validations (four charts, two values of $\lambda$, twenty systems)
satisfied \eqref{eq:val}, with mean validated ARL$_0$ of 496.5--502.2 at $\lambda=0.1$. For the oracle, whose in-control scores are independent, a Markov-chain approximation
\citep{brook_evans_1972,lucas_saccucci_1990} gave a mean ARL$_0$ of 501.7 at the calibrated limits. A common ARL$_0$ did not mean a common early false-alarm rate: 12.5\% of the conventional chart's paths
signalled before day 50, against 9.5--10.4\% for the other charts and 9.3\% under a geometric run length, and an
exploratory analysis found its run length over-dispersed (coefficient of variation 1.04), whereas GARCH and the oracle
were close to geometric.

\paragraph{Detection.} RF--EWMA detected the persistent increases more slowly than the conventional chart and slightly
faster than GARCH--EWMA (Figure~\ref{fig:delay}). As $\delta$ rose from 1.5 to 3, $\RMD$ fell from 205.3 to 63.0 days
for the conventional chart, from 243.3 to 126.4 for RF--EWMA and from 249.1 to 139.6 for GARCH--EWMA, with the same
ordering at $\lambda=0.2$. Censoring affects these restricted means: at $\delta=1.5$, 21\% of the surviving GARCH paths
and 20\% of the RF paths had no alarm within 500 days, against 12\% for the conventional chart. Early detection followed
the same pattern: $X(20)$ was 0.054, 0.126 and 0.282 for the conventional chart and about 0.045, 0.100 and 0.220 for
both RF and GARCH.

\paragraph{Adaptation masking.} Figure~\ref{fig:profile} shows how the scores responded. On the day of the change every
chart's mean score rose by the factor $\delta$; the conventional score stayed there, the GARCH and oracle scores fell within a
few weeks to a plateau, and the RF score fell less far. The sustained masking ratio \eqref{eq:masking} was 0.342, 0.308
and 0.281 for RF--EWMA at $\delta=1.5$, 2 and 3, against 0.243, 0.198 and 0.144 for GARCH--EWMA and 0.230, 0.186 and
0.134 for the oracle: the forest, with its attenuated and saturating response, retained more of the shift, increasingly
so for larger shifts. The approximation \eqref{eq:RG} predicted 0.171, 0.134 and 0.094 for the fitted GARCH filters,
0.05--0.07 below the observed values: right in direction but not in size.

Table~\ref{tab:contrasts} gives the twelve primary contrasts. The RF$-$conventional contrasts are large and consistent:
RF--EWMA took 37.5, 60.4 and 63.6 days longer to detect the change at $\delta=1.5$, 2 and 3, in all 20 systems, and its
excess detection within 20 days was lower by 0.008, 0.025 and 0.061. All six remain significant after the supplementary
Holm adjustment (adjusted $p\le9.2\times10^{-5}$). Their no-change counterpart, $-3.80$ days [$-6.53$, $-1.07$], is not
zero, consistent with survivor selection after the conventional chart's more frequent early alarms; the
excess-detection contrasts remove this baseline.

The RF$-$GARCH contrasts are smaller and less certain. RF--EWMA was faster on average by 6.05, 9.94 and 13.13 days, with
unadjusted intervals that exclude zero, but no contrast survives the Holm adjustment (adjusted $p=0.053$ at each
$\delta$), and only 14 or 15 of the 20 systems favoured RF. The standard deviation of the system-level contrasts, 9.4,
15.3 and 20.8 days, far exceeds the within-system Monte Carlo standard error of about 2.7 days, and at $\delta=3$ the
contrasts ranged from $-46.8$ to $+18.3$ days. The $X(20)$ contrasts were close to zero (Holm $p=1$). In
exploratory analysis each system's contrast was stable across $\delta$ and $\lambda$ (rank correlations 0.85--0.97) and
tracked its RF$-$GARCH difference in masking ratio (rank correlations $-0.80$ to $-0.89$): systems whose forest
retained more of the shift detected it sooner.

\paragraph{Discussion.} By forecast accuracy the charts rank oracle $\approx$ GARCH, then RF, then conventional; by speed
of detecting persistent increases the order is reversed. A constant denominator turns a $\delta$-fold variance increase
into a $\delta$-fold increase in the expected score for as long as the change lasts, whereas an adaptive denominator
raises the score only briefly and then absorbs much of the change. The better a forecaster tracks the current variance,
the more it absorbs, so a forecast-standardised chart monitors surprises relative to recent volatility rather than its
level; the forest's attenuated response, a weakness for forecasting, leaves more of a persistent shift visible. This
explanation is consistent with the evidence but not proven by it, because the charts also differ in score dependence,
run-length shape and pre-change false alarms. The GARCH-generated data also favour the GARCH forecaster, and other
changes, such as transient shocks, may rank the charts differently. Within these limits RF--EWMA is not superior to the conventional
chart for persistent increases, and its advantage over GARCH--EWMA is modest and system-dependent.

\paragraph{GARCH specification and calibration.} The unrestricted GARCH(1,1)-$t$ fit had persistence 0.9976, an implied
unconditional variance of 1.92 against $s^2=1.144$ and a fourth-moment coefficient \eqref{eq:m4} of 1.009; it failed the
pre-specified finite-fourth-moment check, and the analysis stopped before any limit was calibrated or any monitoring
return used. On training-data grounds it was then replaced by a variance-targeted GARCH(1,1)-$t$, a method attributed
to \citet{engle_mezrich_1996}, with $\omega=s^2(1-\alpha-\beta)$ and $(\alpha,\beta,\nu)$ estimated by maximum
likelihood, at a cost of 0.24 log-likelihood units. This model has a finite variance ($\alpha+\beta=0.9955$) but an
infinite fourth moment (coefficient 1.0039). Variance targeting is consistent without moment conditions, but its
asymptotic normality needs a finite fourth moment, which \citet{francq_etal_2011} find questionable for daily index
returns, and their theory uses a Gaussian rather than a Student-$t$ likelihood; no standard errors are therefore
reported. The model is the GARCH chart's forecaster and also the in-control law for calibration
(Section~\ref{sec:calib}), which favours the GARCH chart. Because the fourth moment is infinite, the target was the
restricted mean $\E[\min(T,5000)]=500$, which is neither $\E(T)$ nor the simulation's $\RMD$. All six limits passed
\eqref{eq:val} (Table~\ref{tab:spyfit}B), although some validation intervals exclude 500 (RF at $\lambda=0.1$: 477.4
[456.5, 498.3]) and two calibrations at $\lambda=0.2$ overshot the target by slightly more than a pre-set 0.1\% warning
threshold; both were recorded, not adjusted.

\paragraph{Interpretation.} QLIKE against $r_t^2$ (0.915 for GARCH, 0.986 for RF and 1.462 for the constant) repeats the
simulation's ordering, although these proxy-based values are not comparable in level with the simulation's. The RF
denominator never exceeded 3.37, a value it reached on 13 days, whereas the GARCH denominator peaked at 27.4 on
29 October 2008 (Table~\ref{tab:spymon}A): forest predictions are averages of training targets and could not follow the
spikes of 2008 and 2020, which plausibly explains the RF chart's longer episodes then, although an observational
application cannot identify causes. All results here

methods, and the reference events were chosen with hindsight. The long, heterogeneous training period, the high
persistence and the assumption that the fitted GARCH-$t$ model is the in-control law further limit interpretation.

\section{Conclusion and recommendations}\label{sec:conclusion}
This study asked whether standardising squared returns by a random-forest variance forecast gives a useful EWMA chart
for persistent volatility increases. The RF--EWMA chart was well behaved but was not the best detector. The forest
produced positive, stable forecasts that closed 85\% of the accuracy gap between a constant and the true in-control
model, although they responded only weakly to the true variance, and all limits validated at the common in-control run
length of 500 days. Yet the conventional chart with a constant denominator detected persistent increases fastest,
38--64 days sooner on average than RF--EWMA and in every fitted system. RF--EWMA detected them 6--13 days sooner than
GARCH--EWMA on average, a difference that is not robust to multiplicity adjustment and depends strongly on the fitted
system. The forecast-accuracy ranking is the reverse of the detection ranking, and the masking ratios are consistent
with adaptive denominators absorbing part of a persistent change. The SPY application illustrates the same contrast:
the conventional chart stayed above its limit through long high-volatility periods, while the forecast-standardised
charts flagged shorter episodes around sharp moves.

The main lesson is that choosing the denominator chooses the monitoring target. A constant denominator monitors the level
of volatility and suits regime shifts; a forecast denominator monitors surprises relative to recent volatility and
largely adapts to persistent changes. More accurate forecasts are therefore not automatically better monitors. In
practice the denominator should be matched to the change of interest, for instance by running a level chart and a
residual chart side by side, and limits for financial scores should be calibrated and validated by simulation rather
than taken from normal theory, with pre-change false-alarm rates reported alongside ARL$_0$. Future work should study
data-generating processes that do not favour GARCH, such as regime-switching models; transient shocks and changes in
volatility dynamics; a smaller in-control target such as ARL$_0=100$; CUSUM-type charts; forests trained on realised
variance; and control limits that account for calibration uncertainty.
